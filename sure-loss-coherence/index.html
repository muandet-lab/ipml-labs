01 — CONSISTENCY OF BUYING PRICES

No sure loss.
But is it coherent?

A lower prevision is your supremum acceptable buying price for a gamble. Compare one-sided buying commitments with precise, two-sided prices. Set four prices, then discover whether they fit together.

Set your buying prices

Three mutually exclusive outcomes: A, B, C. Each ticket pays €1 if its event occurs, and €0 otherwise.

THE PRICE VIEW

Coherence means every price is tight

Natural extension is the smallest expectation over M. A gap reveals a buying price already implied by your other commitments.

Why these are different requirements

Avoiding sure loss: every nonnegative buying portfolio has a nonnegative payoff in at least one state.

maxω ∑ λᵢ[fᵢ(ω) − P̲(fᵢ)] ≥ 0
for all λᵢ ≥ 0

Coherence: even when you sell one assessed gamble at its quoted price, buying other gambles cannot force a strictly negative payoff in every state.

On this finite domain, coherence is equivalent to P̲(f) = minp∈M Ep[f] for each assessed f. Avoiding sure loss only requires M ≠ ∅.

These prices are limiting buying prices. Strictly acceptable transactions can use prices just below them; strict sure-loss certificates persist under sufficiently small perturbations.

A two-minute experiment

Start with “Avoids sure loss only”: A and B each cost 0.30, but A ∪ B costs only 0.40. Buying A and B implies a price of 0.60 for their sum. Selling A ∪ B at 0.40 while buying A and B loses 0.20 in every state. This violates coherence, even though buying alone cannot force a sure loss.

Then choose “Sure loss” and find a portfolio. Finally, try “Complete ignorance”: coherent prices can leave the entire probability simplex open.
